Village in the Shade · Review

Village in the Shade Review: A Farming Sim You Can Slack Off In, and a Story You Can't Put Down

From the Yomawari team: farming by day, spirits by night. I finished it in about 48 hours and never lost steam.

Bubblegum · Finished in about 48 hours on the Japanese release · September 25, 2026

The ducks eat the weeds in my rice paddies. The sprinklers water the fields. A machine collects the eggs and the milk. Meanwhile, I go fishing in the river.

Village in the Shade is a life sim where you farm and raise animals, a lot like Story of Seasons, the series older players know as Harvest Moon. And yet you can slack off this much.

When I play Story of Seasons, I usually lose steam somewhere. Water every morning, feed the animals, ship, sleep. Partway through, the routine wears me down. In Village in the Shade, the chores leave your hands one at a time before you get bored of them. You spend the time you've freed up fishing or hunting, and while you're out, the village's story starts to move. I played for nearly fifty hours, and I never lost steam.

The farming side is quiet field work and fishing that gets the endorphins going. On top of that, automation and a story that keeps throwing surprises at you add the dopamine. You always want to know what happens next. If I had to sum it up in a phrase, it's a dopamine-driven Story of Seasons.

I played the Japanese release, which came out in July 2026, to the end. The English version came out on October 27. Some names in the English version may differ from what I describe here, so I've described things rather than guessed at their names.

Farming by day, spirits in the village by night

It's made by Nippon Ichi Software, known for Disgaea and Yomawari, and it's a new game from the Yomawari staff. It's on Switch, Switch 2, PS5 and PC.

The hero gets lost in the mountains, loses their memory, and is rescued by the villagers. They might be a villager who went missing here, but nobody can confirm who they are. For now, they borrow a corner of the village and start living off the land.

The strange thing is that, for people who saved you, the villagers won't really talk to you. You're given a code of eight rules, like don't go out after 11 at night and don't dig into other people's secrets, and at night, strange things start happening in the village.

By day, you farm, raise animals, fish and hunt. At night, you walk a village where spirits appear, and it becomes a horror game. The play changes completely between day and night, so the days never feel like the same thing over and over.

The fear comes in two kinds: a slow, creeping unease, and sudden jump scares while you're exploring at night. Both are mild for a horror game. If you're not good with horror, you can still get through with a little nerve. It felt like Yomawari with the difficulty turned down.

If horror really isn't for you, there's a Village in the Sunshine mode with no horror at all. But the night village is a big part of what makes this game, so if you can, play in the normal Village in the Shade mode.

The more you unlock at the shrine, the more you can slack off

At first you do everything by hand. Till with the hoe, sow seeds, water with the can every morning. Feed the animals, collect the eggs. Run out of stamina and you can't farm or fish any more. So far, it's a typical Story of Seasons.

Things change once you repair the shrine at the crossroads. Offer magatama, the comma-shaped jewels, and you unlock abilities: sprinklers, processing machines, how to make fences and garden flooring. There are also bonuses that make villagers and animals warm to you faster, and ones that raise your shipping prices.

It branches like a skill tree. The small sprinkler leads to a normal sprinkler, then an upgraded one, and every time you restore the shrine, more of the tree opens up. You also find magatama and sprinklers on late-night exploration, so the more scared you get walking the village at night, the easier the farm gets by day. Day and night really do connect.

For the animals, machines take over collecting eggs and milk.

Then there are the rice paddies, which I love. Use a plough and you get a paddy where you can grow rice and wasabi. Keep ducks, and they eat the weeds that come up in your paddies and fields. Weeds appear a while after you plant, and normally you'd have to go round cutting them with a sickle. You can leave it to the ducks.

As you work out how to lay out paddies and how many ducks to keep, the work you do yourself shrinks visibly. Working out how to slack off was so much fun that it was what brought me back to the village the next day.

An update made harvesting easier too. Unlock the upgraded sickle at the shrine and you can cut crops in one sweep. Sowing is still by hand, but some crops, like rice, give several harvests from one planting, and some grow across seasons, so it doesn't bother me much.

In Story of Seasons, automation always felt weak to me. Make your fields bigger and your morning chores grow with them. In Village in the Shade, if you set things up well, bigger fields don't mean much more work.

With the time you save, you go fishing and hunting. Fishing in particular is a joy! The fish change with the seasons, so every new season sends you back to the river.

For hunting, you buy a license at the hunter's hut and catch animals with a bow or traps. Some animals fight back, so you dodge their attacks and shoot in the gaps. There's real action between farming days.

No romance, no marriage, and you still want to know what happens

Story of Seasons usually means romance and marriage. You get close to someone in the village, marry them, and live together. Village in the Shade doesn't have that. There's friendship with villagers, and raising it triggers events for each of them, but nobody becomes your partner and you don't get married.

Even so, I never lost steam. Story events come often, and they're all genuinely good. Before you get tired of farming, the next part of the story arrives.

A small spoiler for the early game follows. I don't touch the heart of the story from the middle onward. Please play that for yourself.

Not long after you arrive, the chickens in the coop are killed. If you started this thinking it was a peaceful farming game, that's not something you'd expect.

I've seen people say the early game is boring. For me it was the opposite. The game made me think "something's wrong with this village" very early on, and I was excited from then on. The more you think it's a peaceful game, the more it'll surprise you, in a good way.

Around the middle, the story suddenly picks up and the threads it's been laying down start to pay off properly. There are moving scenes, it wraps up beautifully, and the aftertaste is wonderful. The story is what I rate highest in this game.

The characters change over the course of the story too. The villagers who wouldn't talk to me at first were people I loved, every one of them, by the end.

About 48 hours to finish, and what's left after

It took me about 48 hours to finish the story. Moving briskly, you could probably do it in under forty.

There's still plenty to do afterward. Fill the village head's deliveries, unlock the rest of the shrine's abilities, collect machine parts, old rat coins and lost books, and complete the field guide. If you want to go deep, there's a lot here, so I don't think it'll feel short.

You can decorate your house however you like, too.

The music is excellent. It suits the village perfectly, and it changes with the season and the weather. It's catchy enough that I listen to the soundtrack on Spotify now.

That said, finishing the story doesn't add new places or new enemies. Farming, mining and hunting are all in place by the middle of the story, so after the ending it tended to become the same work on repeat. Honestly, I wanted a little more to do.

More is on the way. In Japan, free updates have added things like a furniture catalog, more farmland, camping furniture, new animals and a birthday event for the hero, and a paid expansion with a new story and a new map has been announced. That should fill the gap after the ending, little by little.

What bothered me

Almost everything that bothered me has to do with time.

  • Fast travel only goes home. To reach houses and mountains at the far end of the map, you walk every time.
  • Getting around eats your day. Walking from one end of the map to the other uses a lot of in-game time. Unless you decide what each day is for, hunting one day, fishing the next, villagers' requests the day after, the day is gone in a moment.
  • Waiting for shops to open. You can be up at 7 a.m., but shops and the library open at 9 or 10. On days you want to get errands done first thing, you end up waiting.

My verdict: a must-play

I've never played a Story of Seasons-style game with a story this rich. Hard to believe it's from the company that made Disgaea.

I'd especially recommend this grown-up Story of Seasons to anyone who loses steam partway through farming games, and to anyone who loves building systems that let them slack off. If you like Yomawari, it's an absolute must. Every character has real personality, and there's a story that makes you care about them. If you're mainly looking forward to romance and marriage, the usual heart of the genre, it may not be for you.

And if you do play it: set up the ducks, set up the sprinklers, and go fishing.
