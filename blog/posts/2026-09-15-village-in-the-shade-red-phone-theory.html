Whose Voice Is on the Red Phone? A Village in the Shade Theory

Line the voice up against the Lost Books, and it leads to a man who was sent into the pit not that long ago.

Bubblegum · After finishing the game · September 15, 2026

On rainy nights, the phone booth at the edge of the village is the only thing with its light on.

The red phone I'd picked up started ringing, so I ran all the way there, and when I answered it inside the booth, a man's voice came through. There was so much static I couldn't catch half of it, but I could tell he was trying hard to say something.

Whose voice is it?

Line it up against the library books lying around the village, and it leads to a man from the village head's family. And not the man from the Meiji era who appears in the books. Most likely someone from the 1990s.

Spoilers for the red phone event and for what the Lost Books say. I've finished the game, but this post doesn't touch the ending. How to reach the booth and get through the event is in a separate guide.

A broken voice, in five parts

The static eats parts of every line, so I filled them in one at a time from the parts I could read. Put together, it goes something like this:

No time left. As a member of the family, he has to do his duty, and he can't run away. He's made up his mind, but he can't bear what he's leaving her to. So please tell her he did his duty well. He always loved her. He's sorry it ended like this.

There are two clues. His family name, and the word "duty." He says "duty" twice, and both times it sounds like something that belongs to the family, not to him. So I checked both against the library books.

His family is the village head's family

One of the Lost Books is a record of the village's sacrifice ritual, dated in the Meiji era, over a century ago. In a year that marked ten years, a twenty-year-old man named Hiroki was chosen as the sacrifice "as is customary," and the ritual went smoothly. His last words were that he would carry out the duty of the village head's family well.

Hiroki's family name is the same as the man's on the phone. In Japanese it's written 樹木, "trees." Another book, a will, shows the village head's seat being passed on inside that same family. The person writing the will and the person named as the next head both have that name.

So that family is the village head's family. The "duty" the man on the phone keeps mentioning is becoming the sacrifice.

One from the head's family, every ten years

Where it started is in another book, about a human sacrifice that calmed a disaster.

Disasters kept falling on the village. Offering vegetables from the fields didn't stop them, and neither did offering horses, so the choice fell on the village head's two daughters. The older sister stepped forward to protect the younger one, and the disasters stopped for a while. Ten years later, rain and trees attacked the village again, and this time the younger sister was offered too.

That's where the "year that marked ten years" in the Meiji record must come from. Every ten years, one person from the head's family. Man or woman, it didn't matter.

Same two words as the Meiji record

This is where I noticed it. Hiroki's last words and the man on the phone say the same thing.

WhereWhat's left
The ritual record (Meiji era)For the village to go on, I'll carry out the duty of the head's family well
The voice in the phone boothI did my duty well

"Well" and "duty." The same two words.

The voice is a man. The one who comes out at night is a woman.

The voice on the phone is a man's. But the spirit that chases you around the village at night is a woman with wild hair.

She shows up on the screen during the call, too. The glass is stained red and you can't see out from inside, but while the man's voice was playing, she was standing just on the other side of it.

So there are two people in this one event: the man left in the phone, and the woman left in the village. He says he always loved her. Since his voice and her spirit come out in the same event, I think she followed him after he became the sacrifice.

Not a smartphone. A mobile phone.

So when did this man live? Because Hiroki and the man on the phone use the same words, at first I thought the voice was Hiroki's.

But then the phone doesn't fit.

What you pick up in the village is a bright red mobile phone. Not a smartphone. Zoom in on the icon and it's not even a flip phone. It's a straight candy-bar phone with an antenna sticking out, and its description calls it an old-looking phone.

There were no mobile phones in the Meiji era. In Japan, people only started carrying their own phones in the Heisei era, which began in 1989, and this shape was normal in the 1990s. Whether the phone was his or hers, they lived when people walked around with phones like this.

Two people born in completely different eras say the same words right before they become the sacrifice. I don't think that's a coincidence. People in that family were taught to say it, generation after generation. It's close to brainwashing.

From the end of the Meiji era to the early 1990s is more than seventy years. At one every ten years, there must have been at least seven more between Hiroki and the man on the phone, each saying the same line before going down into the pit.

Putting it together

  1. The man belongs to a family with a duty he can't escape.
  2. That family is the village head's family, which has given a sacrifice every ten years.
  3. He uses the same words as the Meiji sacrifice: "I did my duty well."
  4. He lived when people carried phones like that, most likely the 1990s. The ritual went on that long.
  5. He became the sacrifice, and tried to leave a message for the woman he was leaving behind.
  6. She followed him, and became the spirit that walks the village at night.
  7. The event only happens once, so hearing his voice to the end let her rest.

Number 7 needs a reason. Before the call, she's out in the village at night and chases you. After the call, she stops appearing, and the event never repeats. So I think the message got through once someone listened all the way to the end.

It didn't have to reach her in person. Someone just had to hear the whole thing. For a long time, nobody picked up.

The voice belongs to a man from the village head's family who became the sacrifice around the 1990s. I don't know his name. He was born generations after Hiroki, said the same words, and went to do the same duty.

This isn't a story from a hundred years ago. That family still runs the building company in the village, and not that long ago, a man from the same family as the people you talk to every day was sent into the pit.

Night in the village isn't one point in time

One more thing bothers me: the first line of the call. He says he's out of time.

From where I stand, he died long ago. But on his end, he's just about to go and die. The two ends of one call aren't on the same timeline.

The spirits in the Lost Books start with "long, long ago," events from hundreds of years back, and yet the people in those stories walk outside at night. Then a phone from the 1990s rings on top of that. Hundreds of years of nights are lined up in one night.

That changes how I read the first rule of the code: no going out after 11 p.m. I'd read it as "it's dangerous out there." But if night is a different time altogether, it's less "don't go out" and more "don't go there, it's another world."

On rainy nights, that phone booth is the only thing with its light on, probably for the same reason. That one night in the 1990s still hasn't ended there.
