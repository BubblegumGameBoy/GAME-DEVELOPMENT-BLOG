Is Pokémon Pokopia Boring? A Review After Six Months

I've played it since launch and I'm still playing. Here's why some people bounce off it, who it's actually for, and how it compares with Animal Crossing and Dragon Quest Builders 2.

Bubblegum · Playing since March · March 7, 2026

In Pokémon Pokopia, a house takes about fifteen minutes to build. Repairing the Pokémon Center takes until the next day.

That waiting is the whole review, really. I've been playing since the game came out in March. At thirty hours I thought it was great without any reservations, and six months later I'm still picking it up. I also understand exactly why some people call it boring, because the reason is easy to point at.

Why some people find it boring

It's how the rewards come. Pokopia doesn't hand you something every few minutes. Buildings take real time. There's no battling, so there are no numbers going up to make you feel stronger. And for all the talk before launch about a darker world, the main story doesn't lean on that very hard.

It isn't the kind of game where you keep playing because the next unlock is two minutes away. It's the kind where you keep moving things around and suddenly the evening is gone. If you buy it expecting the first kind, you'll probably be bored within three hours.

My own take: as a game, Dragon Quest Builders 2 has more bite. Its story pushes back, and what you build actually does something. Pokopia doesn't have that tension, and it was made with a team that worked on Builders 2, so the comparison is fair.

But they're good at different things. In Builders, the fun is that what you build works. In Pokopia, the fun is that you can sit and look at what you built. If you want the first and buy Pokopia, "boring" is the word you'll end up using. It goes the other way too. If you just want to potter around and buy Builders, the fights get in the way.

What only Pokopia can do

This is why I keep recommending it. There's no other sandbox where you place Pokémon yourself. A tank for a Gyarados, an entire aquarium, a zoo. That only works because you have both the building blocks and the Pokémon, and nothing else gives you that.

The first thing I built was a zoo. The Gyarados tank looked better than I expected, and I've been playing that way ever since.

Some players go much further. When I visited an island where someone had built a whole aquarium, I had to admit they'd beaten me on polish. Others use the building tools to make a game inside the game. There's an island that recreates Only Up, and at that point it isn't really a town-building game any more. Both are in my list of Cloud Islands worth visiting.

Whether this kind of play interests you is probably what decides it. If it does, the slow main story won't bother you. If you buy it thinking the game ends when your town is finished, you'll be bored in three days.

What kind of game it is

You play as a Ditto that has taken the shape of a human trainer. The world where people and Pokémon used to live together has fallen into ruin, and the only one left to greet you is Professor Tangrowth. The goal is to build a place where everyone can gather again.

The idea that holds it together is that Pokémon moves are your building tools. You grow grass with a move from Bulbasaur, soak dry ground with Squirtle's Water Gun, and turn into a Lapras to cross rivers. The more you play, the more you can reach and the more you can make.

Habitats

The heart of the game is the habitat system. Put four patches of tall grass together, or arrange rocks in a certain pattern, and you've made a habitat. The Pokémon that like it start turning up on their own. You can build them anywhere, so they become part of how you lay out your town.

Learning moves

Pokémon you meet teach you their moves, and each one widens what Ditto can do. Water Gun to bring dry land back, Cut for trees, Rock Smash to reshape the ground. Making the hero a Ditto really pays off here. Also, the Ditto is far too cute, and it has turned me into a Ditto fan.

Building and waiting

You gather materials, craft items at a workbench, and build with the Pokémon helping. Buildings finish in real time, about fifteen minutes for a house and until the next day for the Pokémon Center, so you're always doing something else while you wait.

Making Pokémon comfortable raises the town's Environment Level, which opens more challenges and more things to buy, which makes you want to make things more comfortable again. That loop is what keeps you playing for a long time. The clock in the game follows the real one, and there are daily challenges, so a long session and a quick one on the train both work.

Can you play it offline?

Yes, the main game works offline.

What you're doingInternet
Playing the main game aloneOnly for the first download
GameShare or local wirelessNot needed
Cloud Islands and other online playNeeded, plus Nintendo Switch Online

My physical copy is a game-key card, so the game itself isn't on the card. The first time you start it, the console downloads over 10GB. After that, the card is enough and it starts with no connection at all. If you're taking it on a trip, do that first download at home.

What I liked, and what I didn't

Liked:

  • The Pokémon move and react in small, detailed ways. I can just watch them.
  • The music is excellent, and it changes as your town grows. It's Pokémon music arranged in a way that feels close to Animal Crossing, and I love it.
  • New Pokémon arrive at a good pace early on, right before you'd get bored.
  • The map is big.
  • The tutorial is careful enough for people who rarely play games.

Didn't:

  • Building takes real time.
  • The early game holds a lot back, and it takes a while before you see how big it is.
  • Guests in multiplayer can't take items back to their own save.
  • Some request conditions are unclear. One counted rocks hidden underground.
  • If you want battles or training, there's nothing here for you.

My wife played for six hours straight

My wife hardly ever plays games. When she started Pokopia, she couldn't put it down for about six hours. Her words: "I looked up and it was night." It isn't hard, there's always something you want to do next, and the Pokémon are adorable. She said it feels a lot like Animal Crossing. To be fair, her Animal Crossing save is past a thousand hours, so she may not count as a casual player.

Animal Crossing, Minecraft or Builders?

This is the question I get most.

GameIn commonThe big difference
Animal Crossing: New HorizonsSlow pace, neighbour requests, real-time clockPokopia gives you more building freedom, a bigger map and more of a story to finish. The closest match overall.
MinecraftGathering, building, craftingMinecraft is about making anything from nothing. Pokopia is about raising a place with Pokémon, and it's much easier to get into.
Dragon Quest Builders 2Town building with a story, and some of the same developersBuilders has combat and a tougher story. Pokopia has no battles and stays relaxed.

The shortest way to put it is a Pokémon Animal Crossing that a whole family can enjoy, with a much bigger map and much deeper building than Animal Crossing.

When the main story runs out

The online mode opens early. Up to four players share a Cloud Island, and anyone can drop in at any hour, even when the owner is offline. The first thing I did on ours was punch a friend. There's no HP, so it does nothing. After that I built a hot spring, fenced Pokémon in to work for free, and dug a Pokémon prison in the basement.

The main town-building does come to an end, and what you do after that decides how long the game lasts for you. The quickest way back in is to visit islands other people have built. You can walk into a stranger's island at any hour, and the clever ones send you home wanting to build something. Any copy plays in nine languages

My copy is the Japanese release, and it still asks which of nine languages you want to play in: Japanese, English, French, Italian, German, Spanish (Spain), Korean, and Chinese in traditional and simplified characters.

My wife has played in English from the start with no trouble. The requests use plain, everyday words, and the same pattern of asking, doing and thanking repeats constantly, so phrases stick. If you're learning a language, I think playing in it once you know the game is a good way to use it.

The title has a language joke in it too. "Poco a poco" means "little by little" in Spanish and Italian, and it's a term in music as well. Pokopia is named after it.

Little by little

That's the game. The house takes fifteen minutes, the Pokémon Center takes until tomorrow, the town fills up with Pokémon a few at a time. If you need something to happen every two minutes, it isn't for you. If you can enjoy watching a place come together slowly, it suits you completely. Six months on, I'm still playing.
